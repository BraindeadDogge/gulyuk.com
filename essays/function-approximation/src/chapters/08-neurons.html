Chapter 8

Neurons: bricks you can tune

So far the building blocks were fixed in advance: powers, cosines, hat functions. A neural network changes one thing. Each brick comes with its own knobs, and the knobs are part of what gets fitted.

The classic brick is a squashed S-curve, the sigmoid. Four knobs bend it into any rising or falling step you like.

Now add many such bricks. Each one contributes one step at its own position, with its own height. A sum of steps is a staircase, and a fine enough staircase hugs any curve.

Replace the step by a ReLU, which is zero up to a point and then climbs with a constant slope. Each neuron now contributes one kink instead of one step, and the sum is a piecewise-linear curve. With the same number of neurons the error is far smaller: this is the same family of curves as the straight splines of chapter 3, but with knots that a network is free to move.

One honest caveat. Here the positions and heights are read off the target by a formula. A real network finds them by training, which can get stuck or generalise badly. The demo shows what a network could represent, not what training will find.

Math (skippable): the network as a formula

The sigmoid is \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})\), and the single neuron of the first figure is \(y = w'\,\sigma(wx+b) + b'\). Add \(N\) of them and keep one output bias, and you have the whole two-layer network:

$$\hat y = b_{\text{out}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N} w^{\text{out}}_i\, \sigma(w_i x + b_i).$$

Make a neuron steeper (\(w_i \to \infty\), keeping its switching point \(x_i = -b_i / w_i\) in place) and the sigmoid turns into the step \(\theta\), which is 0 for negative arguments and 1 for positive ones. The sum is then a staircase, \(\hat y = b_{\text{out}} + \sum_i w^{\text{out}}_i\,\theta(x - x_i)\). Multiply a step by its own argument and you get the ReLU; multiply the sigmoid by its argument and you get its smooth cousin, SiLU:

$$\mathrm{ReLU}(x) = x\,\theta(x), \qquad \mathrm{SiLU}(x) = x\,\sigma(x).$$

With several inputs the weights are stacked into matrices, one column of \(W_{\text{in}}\) per neuron. Then, for one input vector \(x\) or for a whole batch \(X\) with one sample per column,

$$\hat y = b_{\text{out}} + W_{\text{out}}^{\top}\,\sigma\!\big(W_{\text{in}}^{\top} x + b_{\text{in}}\big), \qquad Y = b_{\text{out}} + W_{\text{out}}^{\top}\,\sigma\!\big(W_{\text{in}}^{\top} X + b_{\text{in}}\big).$$

A linear combination of sigmoids is the whole system: the only nonlinear step is \(\sigma\), applied to one neuron at a time.

Universal approximation (Cybenko 1989, Hornik 1991): a network with one hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any continuous function on a bounded interval. It says nothing about how many neurons, or how to find them.
Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (Liu and colleagues, 2024) move the learnable part from the nodes to the edges: every edge carries its own one-dimensional spline, and nodes simply add. The idea echoes this chapter, a sum of tunable one-dimensional pieces. Whether they match ordinary networks at equal cost is still debated, so treat them as an open question, not a verdict.
