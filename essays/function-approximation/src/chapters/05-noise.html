Chapter 5

Noise: when a perfect fit is a mistake

So far most dots lay exactly on the curve. Real measurements do not. In the figure below every dot is the true curve plus random noise, whichever target you chose in the bar. The ink-coloured line is still the truth, and the fit never sees it.

The model is the polynomial from chapter 1, in the well-behaved Chebyshev basis, with one slider. Filled dots are what it trains on. Hollow dots are fresh measurements it has never seen.

The best degree sits at the bottom of that U. To its left the fit is too stiff to follow the curve, and the error that remains is bias: it would be there however much data you collected. To its right the fit is flexible enough to chase the noise, and the error is variance: a different sample of dots would give a very different curve. A curve that passes through every dot (interpolation) is the extreme case of the second kind.

The readout estimates both. It refits the same degree on {{bv_trials}} fresh samples; bias² is how far the average of those curves sits from the truth, and variance is how much the curves scatter around their own average. On average over samples, the squared test error against the truth is exactly bias² plus variance. The Test RMSE shown is one sample's square root, so do not add it to them directly.

With the Smooth target and the opening sample, degree {{bv_best_deg}} is best, with a test RMSE of {{bv_best_rmse}}. Degree 20 trains down to {{bv_deg20_train}} but tests at {{bv_deg20_rmse}}.

It helps to keep three errors apart. The approximation error asks whether the family of curves can get close at all. The generalisation error asks how much the noise in the data hurts. The optimisation error asks whether the training procedure found the best member of the family. Universality theorems speak only to the first.

Modern networks complicate this picture: some keep improving past the point where they fit the training data exactly. That is an active research topic, and this page does not claim to settle it.
Math (skippable)

For a fit \(\hat f\) trained on a random sample and a fixed point \(x\): \(\mathbb E\big[(\hat f(x)-f(x))^2\big]=\big(\mathbb E\hat f(x)-f(x)\big)^2+\operatorname{Var}\hat f(x)\). Scored against noisy measurements \(y=f(x)+\varepsilon\), a further \(\sigma^2\) is added that no model can remove.

For least squares with \(p\) parameters on \(n\) points the variance averages roughly \(\sigma^2 p/n\), which is why it climbs steadily with the degree.
