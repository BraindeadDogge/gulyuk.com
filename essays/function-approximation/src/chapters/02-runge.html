Chapter 2

Runge's lesson: where you sample matters

Suppose you may measure the function anywhere you like, and you want the one polynomial of degree n that passes through n+1 measurements. The obvious choice is evenly spaced measurements. It is also a trap.

With evenly spaced nodes the polynomial wiggles harder and harder near the ends: at degree 10 the largest error is {{runge_eq10}}, at degree 20 it is {{runge_eq20}}, on a function that never exceeds 1. Adding more data made the fit worse.

Now crowd the nodes toward the ends, spacing them like the horizontal positions of equally spaced points on a circle. These are Chebyshev points. At degree 20 the largest error drops to {{runge_cheb20}}.

No fixed rule for placing nodes works for every continuous function (Faber, 1914). Chebyshev points are simply a very good default for smooth ones.
There is a way out that changes the family: ratios of polynomials, called Padé or rational approximants. The Runge function is itself a ratio, 1 over 1 + 25x², so a rational fit can match it exactly with two parameters where a polynomial needs ever more. The price is that the fit is no longer linear in its parameters, and the denominator can hit zero where you do not want it to.
Math (skippable)

The interpolation error at \(x\) is \(\dfrac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x-x_i)\) for some \(\xi\) in the interval. You cannot control \(f^{(n+1)}\), but you can choose the nodes to keep the product small. Chebyshev nodes (nearly) minimise its maximum; evenly spaced nodes make it huge near the ends.

Equivalently, the Lebesgue constant, which measures how much the interpolant can amplify errors in the data, grows exponentially for equispaced nodes and only logarithmically for Chebyshev nodes.
