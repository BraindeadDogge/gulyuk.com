Chapter 6

Neighbours, stumps and boosting: curves made of steps

Here is a model with almost no mathematics. To predict at a new point, look at the nearest dots and average their heights. That is k-nearest neighbours. The curve it draws is a staircase, because the answer only changes when a different dot becomes one of the nearest.

The second model builds a staircase on purpose. A stump is a single step: one cut position, one height on each side. Boosting adds stumps one at a time, and each new stump is fitted to the residual, the part the sum so far still gets wrong.

Both models are local and piecewise constant. They handle a jump with no ringing at all, which Fourier cannot. They pay for it on smooth curves, where a staircase needs many steps to look like a slope. Averaging more neighbours smooths out noise, and fewer stumps do the same; the extreme settings (one neighbour, or very many stumps) copy the noise.

Boosting is also a first look at a theme that returns in chapter 8: a model made of many simple pieces whose positions are learned, not fixed in advance.

The weakness of neighbours is dimension. Spread points evenly through a unit cube and ask how long a side must be to hold one tenth of them. In one dimension it is 0.1 of the range. In ten dimensions it is {{cube_edge10}}: almost the whole range in every direction. "Nearby" no longer means local. This is the curse of dimensionality, and it is why methods that can ignore useless inputs, such as boosted trees, remain popular on tables of many columns.

Math (skippable)

A stump \(s(x)=\ell\) for \(x\le t\) and \(r\) otherwise, fitted to residuals \(e_i\), chooses \(t\) to maximise \(\frac{(\sum_{\text{left}}e_i)^2}{n_L}+\frac{(\sum_{\text{right}}e_i)^2}{n_R}\); \(\ell\) and \(r\) are the two averages. With learning rate \(\eta=0.5\) the model after \(m\) steps is \(\bar y+\eta\sum_{j\le m}s_j(x)\).

A cube of side \(a\) holds a fraction \(a^d\) of uniform data, so holding a fraction \(p\) needs \(a=p^{1/d}\).
