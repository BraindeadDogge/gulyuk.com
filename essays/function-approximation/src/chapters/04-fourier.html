Chapter 4

Fourier: waves instead of bumps

Splines are local. The opposite idea is to build the curve from waves that fill the whole interval: a constant, then half a cosine wave across the interval, then a full wave, then one and a half, and so on. Add enough of them and a surprisingly wide range of curves comes out.

Unlike powers of x, these waves are orthogonal, which means they do not interfere with each other. Each coefficient is found on its own, by a single integral, and the best fit with K waves is simply the first K+1 terms (the constant plus K waves) of the best fit with more.

Smooth curves need few waves. A jump is another story. Near the jump the partial sums overshoot by about {{gibbs_pct}}% of the jump height, and adding waves only squeezes the overshoot closer to the jump without making it smaller. This is the Gibbs phenomenon. A basis made of global waves cannot make one local discontinuity cheap.

Why cosines on [−1, 1]? Mirroring the function around the end removes the jump a plain sine and cosine series would see there whenever the two end values differ. A kink remains wherever the slope at the end is not zero, and that kink still limits how fast the error shrinks.
Wavelets keep the idea of a set of building blocks but make every piece a small wave that lives in one place and at one scale. A jump then costs a few coefficients near it instead of ripples everywhere, which is why wavelets are used in image compression such as JPEG 2000.
Math (skippable)

We use \(f(x)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{K}c_k\cos\!\big(k\pi\,\tfrac{x+1}{2}\big)\). The cosines are orthogonal on \([-1,1]\), so \(c_k=\dfrac{\int f\,\phi_k}{\int\phi_k^2}\), with \(\int\phi_0^2=2\) and \(\int\phi_k^2=1\) for \(k\ge1\).

The smoother the mirrored extension of \(f\) is, the faster \(c_k\) decays: like \(1/k^2\) if it has a kink at the ends, like \(1/k\) if it has a jump, which is why the ripples never fade.
