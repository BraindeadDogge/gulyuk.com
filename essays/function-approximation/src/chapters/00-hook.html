Chapter 0

Fit it by hand

Below are 24 noisy measurements of some unknown curve. Drag the seven handles to pull a smooth curve through the cloud.

That score is the mean squared error, or MSE. Lower is better. Every method on this page is a different answer to one question: how should the curve be built so that this number is small, and stays small on points it has not seen yet?

That is the whole job. A neural network, a decision tree and a polynomial are all machines for producing curves like this one, built from different simple parts. We will take the parts one at a time.

Math (skippable): the setup in symbols

Every measurement is a pair \((x_i, y_i)\): the circumstances of the measurement and its result. Behind the points sits a dependency we cannot see, \(f\), and what we record is that dependency plus noise \(\varepsilon_i\):

$$y_i = f(x_i) + \varepsilon_i .$$

A model is a function \(\hat y = \mathrm{Net}_w(x)\) with adjustable numbers \(w\), its weights. Over \(S\) measurements its score is the average loss, and training means choosing the weights that make the score smallest:

$$L_{\text{total}}(w) = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} L\big(\mathrm{Net}_w(x_s),\, y_s\big), \qquad w^{*} = \arg\min_{w}\; L_{\text{total}}(w).$$

With the squared distance as the loss this is the mean squared error of the figure above. The absolute distance gives the mean absolute error, which treats a big miss as only proportionally worse:

$$\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \lVert y_s - \hat y_s \rVert^{2}, \qquad \mathrm{MAE} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \lvert y_s - \hat y_s \rvert .$$

A loss is not the same thing as a metric. A metric answers a question you care about, such as "how often is the answer within \(\epsilon\)?":

$$\mathrm{Acc}_{\epsilon} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{i=1}^{S}\big[\,\lvert y_i - \hat y_i\rvert < \epsilon\,\big].$$

It is a fine metric and a useless loss. Nudge \(\hat y_i\) a little and every bracket stays at 0 or 1, so \(\partial\,\mathrm{Acc}_{\epsilon} / \partial \hat y_i = 0\) almost everywhere, and a method that follows slopes gets no signal. A loss has to change smoothly when the prediction moves, and fall as the prediction gets closer to \(y\).

"Fit it for me" runs ordinary least squares with a degree-5 polynomial and reads the handle heights off it.
